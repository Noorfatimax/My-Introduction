The problem

The problem

A pharmacy marketplace only works if every person sees exactly what they should, and can trust everything they can't see.

This platform connects pharmacies, providers (the doctors and clinics who prescribe) and patients, with an admin team running it. Providers collect payment from patients, the platform takes its share, and pharmacies are paid after a short waiting period. The platform sits in the middle, so providers and pharmacies never see each other.

I worked on the three places where trust matters most: who gets to prescribe (verifying providers), what a prescription says (prescription directions), and what can be sold and by whom (medicines). Each one reaches all four roles, and each role has its own app and its own view.

Principles & constraints

Three rules that shaped every screen

01

The platform sits in the middle

People can't open each other's pages. What isn't meant for you is simply missing, not greyed out.

02

Reversible and traceable

Nothing active is deleted. Pause or archive instead, and every admin action carries the admin's name.

03

Three checks before an order

A medicine can be ordered only if the admin team allows it, the pharmacy is active, and it can be sent to the patient's state.

Verifying providers · Admin team

Letting the admin team trust a provider, and undo it safely

Decision 01 · Verification

Four stages, and every step can be undone

A new provider is verified before they can prescribe: the admin team checks their identity and credentials. Each provider is in one of four stages: waiting for verification, active, rejected or paused. Rejecting needs a reason, so the provider can fix it and apply again. Pausing stops new orders and new patients straight away, while orders already accepted are still fulfilled.

I chose pausing over deleting, with a confirmation step, because orders already in progress still have to be fulfilled and every action needs a record of who did it.

Decision 02 · Money

Refunds that stay inside the platform

When a patient is refunded, the money goes to the provider as account credit, and their next order uses that credit first. Cash refunds happen outside the platform, so the admin team adds them as a manual record. Adding a record, making an adjustment and both kinds of refund live in one tab, next to the provider's share.

I chose credit over cash refunds because the provider collects the payment, and every amount has to add up.

Prescription directions · Every role

Directions that are quick to pick and hard to get wrong

Decision 03 · Writing directions

Build a sentence, or write your own, never both at once

Directions are built like a sentence, using dropdowns and number chips, with the right singular and plural, plus one optional note. A template can also be written entirely by hand. Editing the full text switches to hand-written mode and hides the builder, and "Back to builder" brings it back.

I chose one mode at a time over two inputs side by side, because it removes the case where the sentence and the builder disagree.

Decision 04 · While prescribing

One field that searches, narrows down and lets you write your own

When a provider sets up a medicine for a patient, one field lists directions for that medicine, from the pharmacy's ready-made ones and the provider's own saved ones. Typing narrows the list, "+ Directions builder" opens the builder, and if nothing matches, the provider can write their own. Patients only read the result.

I moved from tabs to a single field after trying options with the client, who preferred it. Directions written by hand aren't saved to the library automatically; only the builder saves them.

Medicines · Every role

Deciding what can be sold, and keeping changes safe

Decision 05 · Verification

Checked first, live only once verified

Pharmacies add medicines with their options, prices and directions. Providers can't see or prescribe a medicine until the admin team has verified it. If it's rejected, a reason is required, and the pharmacy can fix it and send it again. A medicine can be ordered only when the admin team allows it, the pharmacy is active and it can be sent to the patient's state.

I chose "allowed unless the admin team says no" over giving access one medicine at a time, because it keeps the list usable while still letting the admin team restrict.

Decision 06 · Shared lists

The admin team owns the lists, pharmacies pick from them

Categories, how a medicine is taken (by mouth, injection, on the skin), its form (tablet, cream) and its packaging are set once by the admin team. They are organised as a tree, with archive instead of delete, renaming in place and a count of medicines using each one. Archiving a parent archives what's under it, and restoring it brings them back.

I chose archive over delete because medicines and orders still refer to these values.

Decision 07 · Changing a live medicine

Keep selling on the old details until the new ones are verified

When a pharmacy edits a live medicine, it keeps selling on the old details while the change waits for the admin team. They see the old and new details side by side, and either verify the change or reject it with a reason. The existing price-change request becomes part of the same flow.

I chose verifying every change because even a small change to a medicine can affect safety. Still open with the client: editing directly versus sending a request.
